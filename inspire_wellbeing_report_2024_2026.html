Part One — Qualitative Analysis

A qualitative analysis of open-text survey responses from the 2024–2026 wellbeing workshop cohorts, examining emotional experience, identity, motivation, and wellbeing needs.

Charlotte Price  ·  Director of Grantee Safety & Wellbeing

Responses were drawn from 15 qualitative fields including emotional experience narratives, goal descriptions, struggle motivators, and facilitation requests. The cohort spans sport, performing arts, academic research, law, and cultural pursuits across Aotearoa New Zealand. Three test submissions have been excluded from the analysis.

Seven themes identified
01
Primary finding · Most prevalent

Performance anxiety as the dominant emotional challenge

Anxiety is the most consistently named unpleasant emotion across the cohort, appearing in some form in the majority of responses. It clusters around three distinct triggers: pre-competition arousal, pressure to perform under observation by selectors or coaches, and the anticipation of high-stakes outcomes. Several respondents describe this as persistent rather than situational, distinguishing it from normal arousal and placing it in a pattern of ongoing, low-level distress. The Advice field makes this the most requested topic for facilitator discussion. One respondent describes tensing side-stage and losing control of their performance despite having tried multiple self-regulation techniques, suggesting this group has encountered general advice but needs more targeted and individualised strategies. This finding aligns directly with the elevated Nervous (2.88) and Jittery (2.35) PANAS items in the quantitative data.

02
Identity

Identity-contingent self-worth and performance-based self-evaluation

A distinct clinical theme emerges around basing self-worth on performance outcomes. One respondent names this explicitly as the topic they most want to address. A musician describes trying to "quieten the negative voice" to process feedback rather than experiencing it as an attack. An Irish dancer reflects on having based their self-worth on competition results for years. Self-doubt, inferiority, and imposter syndrome appear repeatedly across the Unpleasant and Stress fields, concentrated most heavily in the older cohort.

03
Motivation

Commitment and obligation as the dominant motivational frame

The Struggle Motivator responses reveal a clear pattern: external obligation is the most common driver across the cohort. Phrases like "I made a commitment," "my parents have given up too much for me to give up," and "people are relying on me" appear independently across multiple respondents. Fewer than a third use primarily intrinsic language, consistent with the psychometric finding that controlled motivation (3.62) substantially exceeds autonomous motivation (2.37). The practical implication is that uptake of wellbeing support will likely require an external frame to initiate.

04
Transition

Relocation, transition, and structural change as acute stressors

The Stress and Sad fields consistently identify major life transitions as the highest-stress events. These include moving overseas for study or performance programmes, returning from injury, first experiences in unfamiliar environments, and the shift from study to career. A newly identified stressor is the "post-achievement void" — two respondents independently identify the emptiness that follows completing a major goal: "after ticking something off your list, there's always a little sense of sadness afterwards because now that it's been accomplished, what next."

05
Injury

Physical injury as a psychological trigger and identity disruptor

Injury appears as a distinct and recurring stressor. An Irish dancer describes a foot injury as a "major stress spiral" because dance was simultaneously their primary coping mechanism and identity anchor — the injury removed both at once. A clay target shooter describes a shoulder injury of two years' standing that continues to affect competition performance and emotional state. The pattern is consistent: injury does not only cause physical limitation, it disrupts the primary coping structure and introduces sustained uncertainty about long-term capacity.

06
Social support

Social connection as the primary coping resource

Across the Better field, the most prevalent coping strategies are relational. Calling family, talking to teammates, and spending time with those who understand the specific pressures of their pursuit are named far more frequently than any individual technique. Several respondents describe their support network with notable specificity and warmth. The implication for Ignite: when the primary coping resource is relational and a Grant Recipient is removed from it through relocation, the removal of that coping strategy is itself a clinical risk factor warranting proactive rather than reactive support.

07
Attribution

Gratitude and external attribution of success

A quiet but consistent theme runs through the Relief and Happy fields: a high proportion of respondents frame their success in terms of gratitude toward others — sponsors, parents, coaches, grantors, and communities. Combined with the external obligation motivational frame, this pattern suggests a cohort that may struggle to locate success as internally generated. Wellbeing support should include work on attributional style — helping Grant Recipients recognise their own agency alongside the circumstances that enabled them.

Implications for Ignite provider matching
01

Performance anxiety management

The highest-frequency presenting need across the cohort. Providers with experience in sport psychology, cognitive-behavioural approaches to anxiety, and pre-performance arousal regulation are the appropriate match. This applies across pursuit types, not only sport. Priority placement for this provider type in the white-label platform.

02

Identity and self-worth work

Indicated for a meaningful subset of the cohort, particularly older Grant Recipients and those in performing arts and academic fields. Less about crisis intervention and more about building the psychological infrastructure to withstand performance evaluation without self-concept collapse. Psychologists and counsellors with experience in high-performance identity and perfectionism are the appropriate match.

03

Proactive transition support

The most universally applicable offering and the one most likely to achieve uptake given the external obligation motivational frame. A single session focused on planning, anticipatory coping, and social connection strategies before departure from home environment addresses the relocation anxiety theme directly and gets Grant Recipients over the initial engagement barrier with the platform.

Part Two — Statistical Analysis
Why measure, and what are we measuring?

The wellbeing survey administered to each cohort at the start of their Inspire journey was not designed as a clinical screen. Its purpose was to establish a baseline picture of how Grant Recipients are feeling emotionally, how clearly they understand their own identity, and the quality of their motivation — before the pressures and transitions of their grant year take hold. Measured at this point, the data captures not a problem population, but a high-functioning one carrying a particular psychological load.

Three validated scales were used. The PANAS (Watson, Clark & Tellegen, 1988) captures emotional experience across 20 items, separating positive and negative affect as independent dimensions rather than opposites. The EPSI Identity subscale (Rosenthal, Gurney & Moore, 1981) measures Eriksonian identity clarity — the degree to which a person has a coherent, stable sense of who they are. The Self-Determination Scale (Sheldon & Deci, 1996) measures the quality rather than quantity of motivation, distinguishing between autonomous drive that comes from within and controlled motivation driven by obligation and external pressure.

The analyses that follow move from description to comparison to prediction. They are designed to answer three practical questions: what does the emotional profile of this cohort look like; whether that profile differs meaningfully by age or gender; and what predicts distress. Each finding is accompanied by an interpretation of what it means for how Ignite support should be designed and targeted.

Step 1 — Descriptive statistics
Why this step
Before running any inferential tests, it is essential to understand the shape of the data. Descriptive statistics tell us where the cohort sits on each scale, how much variation exists within the group, and whether the scores are distributed normally — which determines which statistical tests are appropriate. The Shapiro-Wilk test is the standard normality check for samples under 50. If a scale passes, parametric tests such as ANOVA and t-tests can be used with confidence. If it fails, non-parametric equivalents are required. Running this step first protects the integrity of every analysis that follows.

What does this mean?
This cohort is emotionally engaged and energised — Positive Affect sits above population norms, which reflects the self-selection of high-achieving young people who have actively pursued a competitive grant process. However, Negative Affect is also above norms, sitting at 2.02 against a population average of approximately 1.5. All four scales pass normality, which means the statistical tests that follow are appropriate and their results can be trusted.
Step 2 — Age group comparisons (one-way ANOVA)

Why this step The Inspire cohort spans a ten-year age range, from 15-year-olds at the start of secondary school to 24-year-olds finishing postgraduate degrees or beginning professional careers. That developmental span is not trivial — it covers the full arc of late adolescence and emerging adulthood, a period in which identity consolidation, emotional regulation, and motivational orientation change substantially. A one-way ANOVA tests whether the differences we observe between age groups are larger than what we would expect from chance alone. If they are, it tells us that age-stratified support is not just intuitively sensible but statistically warranted.

Each scale compared across three age groups (15–17, 18–20, 21+) using one-way ANOVA. Levene's test confirmed equal variances for all variables. Tukey HSD post-hoc reported where F is significant.

What does this mean?
Age matters most for distress and self-clarity. The 21+ group carries substantially more negative affect than younger participants (M=2.40 vs 1.84 and 1.85), and this difference approaches but does not quite reach conventional significance — likely because the sample is too small to detect the effect reliably rather than because the effect is absent. The effect size (η²=.151) is meaningful in practical terms: age group explains around 15% of the variance in negative affect. Identity Clarity is the one formally significant finding, with older participants showing higher scores. This is not straightforwardly reassuring — as the qualitative analysis shows, greater self-awareness does not protect against distress when self-worth is tied to external outcomes. Positive Affect and Motivation do not differ by age, suggesting these are relatively stable across the cohort regardless of life stage.
Step 3 — Gender comparisons (independent samples t-test)
Why this step
Gender is one of the most consistently reported moderators of wellbeing in the adolescent and young adult literature, with females typically showing higher negative affect and greater emotional expressiveness. Testing for gender differences in this cohort allows us to assess whether those patterns hold in a high-performance context, and whether any differences are large enough to warrant differentiated support approaches. The Bonferroni correction is applied because running four simultaneous comparisons increases the risk of finding a spurious significant result by chance — dividing the alpha threshold by the number of tests keeps the overall error rate at the conventional 5%.

Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold: α = 0.0125 (0.05 ÷ 4 tests). Cohen's d reported as effect size. No comparisons survive correction.

What does this mean?
Gender does not produce statistically significant differences in wellbeing once the Bonferroni correction is applied. The one near-significant finding — females scoring higher on Identity Clarity (d=0.74) — is a moderately large effect size and warrants monitoring in future cohorts with larger samples, but should not be over-interpreted here. The absence of a significant gender difference in Negative Affect is notable given the broader adolescent and young adult literature, which typically shows higher NA in females. This may reflect the shared high-performance context flattening gender differences, or may simply reflect insufficient power with n=38.
Step 4 — Correlation matrix (Pearson r)
Why this step
Correlations reveal whether two variables tend to move together across individuals — and if so, how strongly. This step examines the relationships between all four psychometric scales and age, which serves two purposes. First, it provides theoretical validation: if the scales are measuring distinct constructs, they should not correlate too strongly with each other. Second, it identifies which pairings are worth investigating further in the regression model. Pearson r is appropriate here because all scales pass normality. Effect sizes are interpreted regardless of p-value significance, given the constraints of a sample of 38.

What does this mean?
Two relationships stand out. First, Positive Affect and Motivation are significantly correlated (r=.421, p=.009) — participants who feel more motivated also experience more positive emotion day-to-day. This is consistent with self-determination theory: autonomous, enjoyment-based motivation and emotional wellbeing are closely linked. Second, Age and Negative Affect are significantly correlated (r=.357, p=.028) — older participants report more distress, which aligns with the ANOVA finding and the qualitative themes around career transition and identity pressure. The absence of a significant relationship between Identity Clarity and Negative Affect is somewhat surprising but may reflect the small sample size compressing detectable variance. Importantly, Negative Affect and Motivation are not significantly correlated, suggesting that feeling distressed does not necessarily undermine participants' drive to show up — consistent with the qualitative finding that obligation and commitment sustain effort even when emotional experience is difficult.
Step 5 — Linear regression: predictors of negative affect
Why this step
The ANOVA and correlation analyses establish that age is associated with higher negative affect. Regression goes further by asking how much of the variation in distress can be explained by age, and whether psychological variables — specifically identity clarity and motivation — add predictive power over and above it. A two-block hierarchical structure is used deliberately: Block 1 establishes the contribution of age alone, and Block 2 tests whether the psychological scales explain additional variance once age is already accounted for. This structure is more theoretically informative than a single-block model because it separates the demographic effect from the psychological one, showing whether distress in older participants is driven by life circumstances or by something measurable in their psychological profile.

What does this mean?
Age is the strongest individual predictor of negative affect in this cohort, explaining around 13% of the variance on its own. When Identity Clarity and Motivation are added in Block 2, they contribute almost nothing additional (ΔR²=.019), and neither reaches significance. This tells us that age-related distress in this group is not well explained by how clear participants feel about their identity or how motivated they are — it appears to be driven by the life circumstances and pressures that accumulate with age, rather than by psychological profile. For Ignite, this reinforces the case for age-stratified support: older Grant Recipients are not simply a higher-risk version of the younger cohort, they are experiencing qualitatively different stressors that require targeted intervention rather than scaled-up versions of the same approach. Treat this regression as hypothesis-generating given the sample size.
Step 6 — Wellbeing flags

Why this step Group-level statistics are essential for understanding the cohort as a whole, but they can obscure individuals who need immediate attention. Flagging translates continuous scale scores into a practical planning tool: who in this particular intake is carrying enough emotional load that a proactive first contact with Ignite is warranted, rather than leaving uptake to chance? Three flags are applied — elevated negative affect, low positive affect, and a dual-concern flag for those meeting both criteria simultaneously. Fisher's exact tests examine whether flags concentrate in particular demographic groups, which would have implications for targeted outreach.

What does this mean?
Nearly one in three participants shows elevated negative affect, which is a substantial proportion for a group that is not a clinical population. The 21+ age group is disproportionately represented — half of all participants in that bracket are flagged, compared to one in four in younger groups. While Fisher's exact tests do not reach significance given the small cell sizes, the pattern is consistent with every other finding in this report and should not be dismissed on statistical grounds alone. The two dual-concern individuals — flagged for both high negative affect and low positive affect simultaneously — represent the highest-priority cases for proactive Ignite engagement. Both are female and in the 20–21 age range, consistent with the profile that emerges across all analyses. Flagging should be used as a facilitation planning tool, not a clinical assessment.
Step 7 — PANAS item-level means
Why this step
The composite PANAS scores used in the analyses above are averages across ten items each. While useful for comparison, averaging can obscure which specific emotions are driving the group profile. Item-level analysis reveals the texture of the emotional experience — not just that negative affect is elevated, but which emotions in particular are elevated and how they sit relative to the positive affect items. This matters for provider matching: a cohort whose negative affect is driven by shame and hostility requires a different therapeutic approach than one driven by nervousness and jitteriness. What does this mean?

Nervous (M=2.87) is the single most elevated negative affect item and sits higher than Alert (M=2.95), the weakest positive affect item. In practical terms, this means anticipatory anxiety is experienced more consistently than attentional clarity or alertness. The anxiety cluster of Nervous, Jittery, and Upset is the most clinically relevant finding at item level, directly informing the Ignite provider matching recommendation for practitioners experienced in performance anxiety and pre-competition arousal regulation. The low scores on Hostile, Ashamed, and Guilty confirm that the negative affect in this cohort is anxious rather than shame-based or interpersonally directed — an important distinction for selecting therapeutic approaches.
Scale references

Full citations for the three validated psychometric instruments used in this report.

PANAS — Positive and Negative Affect Schedule

Watson, D., Clark, L. A., & Tellegen, A. (1988). Development and validation of brief measures of positive and negative affect: The PANAS scales. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 54(6), 1063–1070. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.54.6.1063

A 20-item measure of positive and negative affective states, rated on a 1–5 Likert scale. The PANAS has demonstrated high internal consistency (PA α=.86, NA α=.87), is largely uncorrelated between subscales, and has been validated across adult and adolescent samples. Population norms: PA ≈ 3.0, NA ≈ 1.5.

EPSI Identity — Erikson Psychosocial Stage Inventory (Identity subscale)

Rosenthal, D. A., Gurney, R. M., & Moore, S. M. (1981). From trust to intimacy: A new inventory for examining Erikson's stages of psychosocial development. Journal of Youth and Adolescence, 10(6), 525–537. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02087944

A self-report inventory assessing Erikson's first six psychosocial stages, developed with 97 adolescents and validated in a sample of 622. The Identity subscale (11 items, rated 1–5) measures Eriksonian identity synthesis and confusion, capturing individual self-knowledge, consistency, and self-continuity. Widely used with adolescent and emerging adult samples.

SDS — Self-Determination Scale

Sheldon, K. M., & Deci, E. L. (1996). The Self-Determination Scale. Unpublished manuscript, University of Rochester.

Grounded in:

Deci, E. L., & Ryan, R. M. (1985). Intrinsic motivation and self-determination in human behavior. Plenum Press.

A 10-item scale measuring individual differences in self-determined functioning, comprising two 5-item subscales: Awareness of Self and Perceived Choice in Actions. Items are presented as forced-choice pairs rated on a 1–5 scale. The SDS reflects Self-Determination Theory's distinction between autonomous and controlled motivation and treats self-determination as a relatively stable personality orientation. Higher scores indicate greater autonomous, internally generated motivation.

Statistical methods reference

Analyses conducted in Python 3.12 using SciPy (Virtanen et al., 2020) and Pingouin (Vallat, 2018). One-way ANOVA with Tukey HSD post-hoc, independent samples t-tests with Bonferroni correction (α=.0125), Pearson correlations, and hierarchical linear regression. Shapiro-Wilk tests confirmed normality for all scales prior to parametric analysis.
